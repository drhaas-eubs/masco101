The NHS National Programme for IT — £10bn, Ten Years, Abandoned

A landmark public-sector IT failure, read as a framework post-mortem

Case studyUnit 6Group work
Core message · Unit 6

A plan is a hypothesis. A risk register is the honesty test.

Learning focus · ILOs 2 & 3 — Project planning, scheduling & risk. ILO 2: Critically assess the roles and responsibilities of a project manager as part of an organization's strategy. ILO 3: Investigate how to select, develop, plan, schedule and measure projects outcomes and risks.

Key frameworks: The Iron Triangle · The Power–Interest Grid · ISO 31000 · Waterfall/Agile/Hybrid · The GPM P5 Standard

Situation

In 2002 the UK government launched the National Programme for IT (NPfIT) in the National Health Service in England. Its central aim was an electronic care record for every patient, alongside electronic prescribing, digital imaging, a national booking system and new network infrastructure. At launch it was described as the largest civilian IT programme in the world.

The early cost estimate for the core contracts was around £6.2 billion. Later reports by the National Audit Office put total costs at around £10 billion. The programme was dismantled from 2011 without delivering its central care-records objective, although some components, including digital imaging and parts of the national network, were delivered and remained in use.

Complication

Several structural decisions were taken early and not revisited. The programme was specified and procured centrally, with limited involvement from the clinicians and local trusts expected to use the systems. Large, long-term contracts were awarded to a small number of suppliers, with terms designed to transfer risk to them, which in practice made change difficult.

Requirements were fixed at a time when the underlying clinical processes varied widely between trusts. On a stakeholder map drawn from formal authority, individual clinicians would have appeared to hold little power. In practice, their reluctance to adopt systems they had not helped to shape became one of the programme's most decisive constraints.

Time compounded the problem. A decade-long, largely fixed-scope programme ran through a period of rapid change in the technology it depended on. Suppliers left or were replaced, and delivery fell years behind plan.

The findings of the National Audit Office and the Public Accounts Committee converge on a small set of causes: scope fixed too early and too centrally, users insufficiently engaged, a delivery approach mismatched to requirement volatility, and governance in which accountability for outcomes was unclear.

Teaching note and framework pairings (facilitator)

Most groups reach the Power–Interest Grid quickly. The deeper point is the difference between formal and effective power: a grid built from the organisation chart misclassifies clinicians. A second strong line is method mismatch — fixed scope over a long horizon with volatile requirements. Insist that groups separate foresight from hindsight: user engagement, requirement variability and contract rigidity were all visible in 2003. Good controls for Assessment 3 include re-mapping stakeholders at each phase gate and a change-control rule that forces explicit Iron Triangle trade-offs.

Suggested pairings: Power–Interest Grid + P5 People (who can stop you) · Iron Triangle + Waterfall/Agile/Hybrid (what was fixed, and whether it should have been) · ISO 31000 (what a working risk process would have caught)
