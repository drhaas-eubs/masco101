Arclight Consulting — The Workflow That Saved 40% and Lost a Client

An efficiency gain, a verification step quietly dropped, and a client who noticed before the firm did

Case studyUnit 8Group work
Core message · Unit 8

Delegate the task. Never delegate the accountability.

Learning focus · ILOs 1 & 3 — Productivity systems & AI workflow judgment. ILO 1: Systematically select key concepts and principles of operations and technology management that need to be implemented to support value creation. ILO 3: Investigate how to select, develop, plan, schedule and measure projects outcomes and risks.

Key frameworks: The Task Delegation Matrix · Delegate–Verify–Own · The Automation Bias Curve · The Attribution & Integrity Ladder

Situation

Arclight Consulting is a 60-person market intelligence firm in Lyon. In late 2025 a senior analyst, Priya Raghunathan, built an AI-assisted workflow for the firm's weekly sector briefings, delivered to eleven retained clients every Monday morning.

The workflow was genuinely good. It ingested a defined source set, produced a structured draft against a fixed template, and flagged claims requiring verification. Priya's rule was strict: every flagged claim was checked against the primary source before publication, which took about forty minutes. Total production time fell from eleven hours to under seven — a 40% saving — and client feedback improved.

Complication

Arclight rolled the workflow out to the whole research team in January. Six analysts adopted it. Within a month, average production time had fallen further, to under five hours. Nobody asked why.

Afterwards it emerged that four of the six analysts had stopped performing the verification step. Not deliberately: the drafts were consistently clean, the flagged claims consistently correct, and the check came to feel like a formality on a Friday afternoon. The workflow had trained its users to trust it.

In March, a briefing for a packaging-sector client stated that a named competitor had opened a facility in Portugal. The claim was flagged; the check was not done. The competitor had only announced that it would evaluate a site in Portugal. The client's strategy team had already presented the claim internally when they found it was wrong.

The client ended a four-year retainer. In the post-mortem the managing partner asked why the verification rule had failed. The honest answer was that it had never been a rule. It had been Priya's personal discipline, rolled out as a tool without the discipline attached, and nothing detected that it had been dropped.

Teaching note and framework pairings (facilitator)

The briefing is high stakes and easy to verify: delegate, but check every time. The failure is automation bias created by a run of correct outputs, combined with a rollout that transferred the tool but not the rule. Strong fixes are structural: the draft cannot be published until each flagged claim carries a source link and a reviewer's initials; a second person spot-checks a sample weekly; the time report shows verification minutes separately, so a sudden drop is visible. These cost minutes, not hours, and protect the saving.

Suggested pairings: Task Delegation Matrix + Delegate–Verify–Own (what to check and who owns it) · Automation Bias Curve (why checking faded) · Attribution & Integrity Ladder (what the client is told)
