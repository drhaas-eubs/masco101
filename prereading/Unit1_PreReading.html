Core message · Unit 1

AI collapses the cost of execution; it raises the price of judgment.

Key framework: The Human Edge Triangle

The argument

You have read about this. You have seen the frameworks. You have sat through the presentations. Then a senior person asks what you think, and your mind is a wall of text: notes, half-remembered articles, three ideas that will not connect fast enough. You start talking, and you are thinking out loud rather than thinking clearly. Most professionals have been in that room.

JD Meier's account of why this happens is uncomfortable and useful. The problem is not that you lack knowledge. It is that your knowledge is stored in a format that does not load fast enough under pressure. Paragraphs are slow; pictures are fast. People who seem to think quickly in meetings are usually not reasoning from scratch. They are pattern-matching to a shape they have seen before.

That is the premise of this course's architecture: sixteen named Slibraries and eighty single-visual frameworks, reviewed before each session the way a surgeon reviews scans before an operation. The aim is not memorisation. The aim is that the shapes are available when the question comes.

The key framework

The Human Edge Triangle

The Human Edge Triangle names three capabilities that remain human when execution is automated. Judgment is deciding what matters and which question to ask. Relationship is earning the trust of other people, which is granted by people and cannot be computed. Responsibility is being accountable for consequences, which cannot be delegated to a model. Every MASCO101 unit works at least one corner of the triangle.

Judgment — choosing the question, not just answering it
Relationship — trust is granted by people, not computed
Responsibility — accountability cannot be delegated to a model

The second premise is harder. Generative AI has made fluent output almost free. A competent memo, a plausible analysis, a well-structured recommendation can now be produced in seconds by anyone. The thing that used to distinguish you at work no longer does. What remains is a narrower and more demanding set of capabilities: choosing the right question, holding the trust of the people in the room, and being the person who answers for the outcome.

Fluency has also created a new risk. A generative model produces text that looks finished, including references that look real. Its errors do not look like errors. Ji et al. (2023) show that fabricated content is often presented with the same confidence as accurate content, which is why the format of an output tells you nothing about its reliability. The professional response is not to stop using the tools. It is to know which kinds of output need checking and to check them in the right way.

A worked example

A junior analyst asks an AI assistant for the regulatory background to a market-entry question and receives a polished paragraph with two references. Judgment asks whether the paragraph answers the client's actual question. Relationship asks what the client will believe about the firm if a reference is wrong. Responsibility asks who signs the report. Applied together, the three corners produce a simple rule: any named source that will reach a client is opened and read before the report leaves the building.

Three takeaways
  • Knowledge stored as prose loads too slowly under pressure; visual frameworks load fast.
  • A Slibrary is a named, curated set of framework thumbnails you review before you need them.
  • Fluent execution is now cheap. Judgment, relationship and responsibility are not.
Two questions to bring to the session
  1. Which corner of the Human Edge Triangle is your strongest today, and what evidence would convince a sceptical colleague?
  2. Think of the last AI output you used. Did you check its substance, its sources, or neither?
