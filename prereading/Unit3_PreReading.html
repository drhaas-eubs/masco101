Root Cause or Convenient Cause?

Key framework: The 5 Whys and the IDEAL Model

The argument

Taiichi Ohno's rule at Toyota was to ask why five times. What is usually missed is the second half of the rule: every answer had to be evidenced, and you were expected to go and look at the actual process rather than reason from your desk. Without that constraint, five whys produces five confident guesses in a row and stops at whichever cause is most convenient for the person asking.

Bransford and Stein's IDEAL model addresses the same failure from a different angle. Their contribution was to separate Identify from Define. Noticing that something is wrong is not the same as stating what the problem is, and much poor problem-solving is a well-executed solution to a problem nobody defined.

Argyris's Ladder of Inference explains why this is hard to catch in yourself. You move from observable data to conclusion through several rungs of selection and interpretation, quickly and invisibly. By the time you hold a view, the intermediate steps are gone, and the view then filters which data you notice next. That is why competent analysts looking at the same finance system can produce incompatible and internally consistent explanations.

The key framework

The 5 Whys and the IDEAL Model

The IDEAL Model is a five-stage cycle. Identify that a problem exists and is worth solving. Define it precisely, with a goal. Explore several strategies before choosing. Act. Look back and evaluate honestly whether the action worked. The 5 Whys sits inside Explore: it drives analysis from symptom to a cause that is inside your control, provided each answer is evidenced.

Identify — a problem exists and is worth solving
Define — state the problem precisely and its goal
Explore — generate strategies before choosing
Act, then Look back — evaluate the effect honestly

Now add a model to the room. Generative AI is very good at the middle rungs: it selects, interprets and produces a fluent conclusion at high speed. It is not good at noticing that the question you asked was the wrong question, because answering the question asked is what it is optimised to do. Parasuraman and Riley's work on automation bias adds the second risk: the more fluent the output, the less closely people check it.

Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 describe the mechanism. System 1 is fast and associative; System 2 is slow and effortful, and is often recruited to justify what System 1 has already concluded. Fluent AI output feeds System 1 directly. The discipline this unit teaches is to force System 2 into the definition step, before any answer is on the table.

A worked example

A café chain sees a fall in afternoon sales. Identify: revenue between 14:00 and 17:00 is down 12%. A quick answer blames a new competitor. Define forces precision: is the fall in transactions or in basket size, and in all stores or some? The data shows it is transactions in six city-centre stores only. The 5 Whys then runs on evidence: fewer customers (till data) → longer queues at lunch spill over (timing data) → one of two baristas moved to a morning shift (rota) → the change was made to cut overtime (HR note). The cause is inside the company's control, and the competitor story was never tested.

Three takeaways
  • The 5 Whys without evidence at each step is five guesses in sequence.
  • Identify and Define are different acts; skipping Define is the dominant failure mode.
  • AI answers the question you asked. Judgment is knowing it was the wrong question.
Two questions to bring to the session
  1. In the Brightfold case, which of the four explanations did you find most convincing on first reading, and what evidence did you use?
  2. What question would you ask the CFO before analysing anything else?
