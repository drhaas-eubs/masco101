1Introduction to Tools for Success & Digital/AI Literacy

Learning focus · ILO 1 · Guided Discovery

Systematically select key concepts and principles of operations and technology management that need to be implemented to support value creation.

Glossary 1.1

Digital Literacy

The confident, critical and responsible use of digital technologies

Digital literacy is the set of knowledge, skills and attitudes that allow a person to use digital technologies confidently, critically and responsibly for learning, work and participation in society. It spans five areas: information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety, and problem-solving, and it treats judgement about technology as part of the competence rather than as an optional extra.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Vuorikari, R., Kluzer, S. and Punie, Y. (2022) DigComp 2.2: The Digital Competence Framework for Citizens. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union.

Glossary 1.2

AI Literacy

Knowing what AI can do, what it cannot do, and how to question it

AI literacy is the set of competencies that enables a person to critically evaluate artificial intelligence, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI systems, and use them as a tool in professional and everyday settings. It combines a working understanding of how AI produces outputs with the judgement to decide when those outputs deserve trust, verification or rejection.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Long, D. and Magerko, B. (2020) 'What is AI literacy? Competencies and design considerations', Proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems. New York: ACM, pp. 1–16.

Glossary 1.3

Generative AI

Systems that produce new text, images, code or audio from learned patterns

Generative artificial intelligence refers to computational systems that learn statistical patterns from large training datasets and use them to produce new content, such as text, images, code or audio, in response to a prompt. Its outputs are plausible continuations of learned patterns rather than retrieved facts, which is why fluency and accuracy must be assessed separately.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Feuerriegel, S., Hartmann, J., Janiesch, C. and Zschech, P. (2024) 'Generative AI', Business & Information Systems Engineering, 66(1), pp. 111–126.

Glossary 1.4

AI Hallucination

Fluent output that is unfaithful to the source or to the facts

An AI hallucination is generated content that is presented fluently and confidently but is nonsensical, unsupported by the input, or factually false, for example an invented citation, statistic or quotation. Because hallucinations look like correct answers, they can only be caught by verification against an independent, authoritative source.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Ji, Z. et al. (2023) 'Survey of hallucination in natural language generation', ACM Computing Surveys, 55(12), Article 248, pp. 1–38.

Glossary 1.5

Source Evaluation

Testing information for currency, relevance, authority, accuracy and purpose

Source evaluation is the systematic appraisal of a piece of information before it is used, commonly organised around five criteria: currency, relevance, authority, accuracy and purpose (the CRAAP test). In an age of generative AI it extends to asking whether a cited source exists at all and whether it actually says what is attributed to it.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Blakeslee, S. (2004) 'The CRAAP test', LOEX Quarterly, 31(3), pp. 6–7.

Glossary 1.6

Automation Bias

Over-reliance on automated output at the expense of one's own checks

Automation bias is the tendency to accept the recommendation of an automated system as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and processing. It shows up as errors of commission, following wrong advice, and errors of omission, failing to act because the system did not flag a problem. It grows with trust, workload and the apparent sophistication of the system.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Parasuraman, R. and Manzey, D.H. (2010) 'Complacency and bias in human use of automation: an attentional integration', Human Factors, 52(3), pp. 381–410.

Glossary 1.7

Experiential Learning

Learning as a cycle of doing, reflecting, conceptualising and testing

Experiential learning is the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience. It is modelled as a four-stage cycle of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation, in which learners must move through every stage for experience to become durable capability.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Kolb, D.A. (1984) Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development. Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice Hall.

Glossary 1.8

Deliberate Practice

Effortful, feedback-rich practice aimed at a specific weakness

Deliberate practice is a structured activity designed specifically to improve current performance, typically targeting a defined weakness, requiring high concentration, and providing immediate feedback. It is distinguished from mere repetition or experience because it deliberately operates at the edge of present ability rather than within the comfort zone.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Ericsson, K.A., Krampe, R.T. and Tesch-Römer, C. (1993) 'The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance', Psychological Review, 100(3), pp. 363–406.

2Know Yourself: Self-Assessment as a Strategic Tool

Learning focus · ILO 1 · Self-Diagnostic Workshop

Systematically select key concepts and principles of operations and technology management that need to be implemented to support value creation.

Glossary 2.1

Self-Awareness

Attending to oneself as an object of evaluation against internal standards

Self-awareness is the capacity to take oneself as the object of attention and to compare one's behaviour, traits and outcomes with internal standards. In professional development it has an internal side, clarity about one's own values, strengths and reactions, and an external side, understanding how one is perceived by others; both are needed for accurate self-positioning.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Duval, S. and Wicklund, R.A. (1972) A Theory of Objective Self-Awareness. New York: Academic Press.

Glossary 2.2

Personal SWOT Analysis

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats applied to a career

A personal SWOT analysis applies the situational-analysis matrix of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats to an individual rather than a firm. Internal factors (skills, habits, gaps) are matched against external factors (labour-market demand, technological change, competition) so that development actions can be derived systematically rather than listed at random.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Weihrich, H. (1982) 'The TOWS matrix — a tool for situational analysis', Long Range Planning, 15(2), pp. 54–66.

Glossary 2.3

Blind Spot

What others see in you that you do not see yourself

A blind spot is the region of interpersonal awareness containing behaviours, habits or traits that are visible to others but not known to oneself. In the Johari Window it is reduced through feedback from others, and it matters professionally because unrecognised patterns shape reputation without the person being able to manage them.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Luft, J. and Ingham, H. (1955) 'The Johari window, a graphic model of interpersonal awareness', Proceedings of the Western Training Laboratory in Group Development. Los Angeles: UCLA.

Glossary 2.4

Personality Type

A preference profile across four dichotomies of perception and judgement

Personality type, in the Myers-Briggs tradition, describes an individual's stated preferences on four dichotomies: extraversion–introversion, sensing–intuition, thinking–feeling and judging–perceiving. It describes preferences, not abilities, and it is used as a language for self-reflection and team conversation rather than as a predictor of performance or a basis for selection.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Myers, I.B. and McCaulley, M.H. (1985) Manual: A Guide to the Development and Use of the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator. Palo Alto, CA: Consulting Psychologists Press.

Glossary 2.5

Behavioural Style (DISC)

Observable working style along dominance, influence, steadiness and compliance

A behavioural style in the DISC model describes how a person tends to act in a given environment along four dimensions: dominance, inducement or influence, steadiness (submission in the original) and compliance. Unlike type models, DISC focuses on observable behaviour and its adaptation to context, which makes it useful for adjusting communication with colleagues.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Marston, W.M. (1928) Emotions of Normal People. London: Kegan Paul, Trench, Trubner & Co.

Glossary 2.6

Growth Mindset

The belief that abilities can be developed through effort and strategy

A growth mindset is the implicit belief that one's basic qualities, such as intelligence or talent, can be developed through effort, good strategies and input from others. It contrasts with a fixed mindset, which treats ability as a static trait, and it predicts how people respond to setbacks, feedback and challenging tasks.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Dweck, C.S. (2006) Mindset: The New Psychology of Success. New York: Random House.

Glossary 2.7

Personal Development Roadmap

A time-bound plan that turns a self-diagnosis into verifiable actions

A personal development roadmap is a structured, time-bound plan that converts the gaps identified in self-assessment into specific development goals, actions, resources and checkpoints. It is distinguished from a wish list by its measurable milestones and by an explicit link between each action and the diagnosed strength or weakness it addresses.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Cottrell, S. (2015) Skills for Success: Personal Development and Employability. 3rd edn. London: Palgrave Macmillan.

Glossary 2.8

Human Edge

The capabilities that remain distinctly valuable when machines automate routine work

In this course, the Human Edge denotes the professional capabilities that complement rather than compete with automation: judgement, self-awareness, adaptability, interpersonal influence and ethical responsibility. The concept draws on the observation that technology substitutes for codifiable routine tasks while raising the value of tasks requiring flexibility, judgement and human interaction.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Autor, D.H. (2015) 'Why are there still so many jobs? The history and future of workplace automation', Journal of Economic Perspectives, 29(3), pp. 3–30.

3Think Better: Critical Thinking & Analytical Reasoning

Learning focus · ILO 1 · Head-To-Head Analysis Lab

Systematically select key concepts and principles of operations and technology management that need to be implemented to support value creation.

Glossary 3.1

Critical Thinking

Purposeful, self-regulatory judgement based on evidence and reasoning

Critical thinking is purposeful, self-regulatory judgement that results in interpretation, analysis, evaluation and inference, together with an explanation of the evidential, conceptual, methodological and contextual considerations on which the judgement is based. It is both a set of cognitive skills and a disposition to use them, including when the answer comes from a machine.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Facione, P.A. (1990) Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert Consensus for Purposes of Educational Assessment and Instruction (The Delphi Report). Millbrae, CA: California Academic Press.

Glossary 3.2

SCQA

Situation, Complication, Question, Answer: the introduction that frames a problem

SCQA is a structure for framing a problem or opening a document: the Situation states what the audience already accepts, the Complication introduces the change that creates tension, the Question is the one the complication raises, and the Answer is the main point. It forces the writer to define the problem precisely before offering a solution.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Minto, B. (1996) The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking. London: Minto International.

Glossary 3.3

MECE

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive: no overlaps, no gaps

MECE describes a way of breaking a problem into parts that are mutually exclusive, so no element is counted twice, and collectively exhaustive, so no relevant element is left out. It is the organising rule behind issue trees and ensures that an analysis covers the whole problem without redundancy.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Rasiel, E.M. (1999) The McKinsey Way. New York: McGraw-Hill.

Glossary 3.4

First Principles Thinking

Reasoning from fundamental truths rather than from analogy or convention

First principles thinking is a method of reasoning that decomposes a problem into its most basic, verifiable elements and rebuilds a solution from them, rather than reasoning by analogy with what has been done before. It exposes hidden assumptions and is particularly valuable when conventional solutions or AI-generated answers merely reproduce existing patterns.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Wertheimer, M. (1945) Productive Thinking. New York: Harper & Brothers.

Glossary 3.5

Design Thinking

A human-centred approach that alternates divergent and convergent thinking

Design thinking is a human-centred approach to innovation that integrates the needs of people, the possibilities of technology and the requirements for business success. It proceeds through iterative phases of discovering and defining the problem, then developing and delivering solutions, alternating divergent exploration with convergent decision-making and testing through prototypes.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Brown, T. (2008) 'Design thinking', Harvard Business Review, 86(6), pp. 84–92.

Glossary 3.6

Cognitive Bias

A systematic deviation from rational judgement caused by mental shortcuts

A cognitive bias is a systematic and predictable error in judgement that arises when people rely on heuristics, such as availability, representativeness or anchoring, to simplify complex decisions under uncertainty. Biases are not random mistakes; because they are systematic, they can be anticipated, exploited by persuasive systems and corrected by structured procedures.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Tversky, A. and Kahneman, D. (1974) 'Judgment under uncertainty: heuristics and biases', Science, 185(4157), pp. 1124–1131.

Glossary 3.7

Dual-Process Thinking

Fast, intuitive System 1 versus slow, deliberate System 2

Dual-process thinking describes two modes of cognition: System 1, which operates automatically, quickly and with little effort, and System 2, which allocates attention to effortful mental activity such as calculation and deliberate reasoning. Many judgement errors occur when System 1 supplies an answer that System 2 fails to check.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Kahneman, D. (2011) Thinking, Fast and Slow. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

Glossary 3.8

Confirmation Bias

Seeking and weighting evidence that supports what one already believes

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek, interpret and remember information in ways that are partial to existing beliefs or hypotheses, while giving less weight to disconfirming evidence. It is amplified when AI tools are prompted in leading ways, because the tool tends to return material that fits the framing of the question.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Nickerson, R.S. (1998) 'Confirmation bias: a ubiquitous phenomenon in many guises', Review of General Psychology, 2(2), pp. 175–220.

4Present & Position Yourself: Personal Branding & Presentation Skills

Learning focus · ILO 5 · Pitch Arena

Critically review the communication process, stakeholder communication and communication in diverse work groups.

Glossary 4.1

Personal Brand

The distinctive promise others associate with you as a professional

A personal brand is the distinctive set of values, capabilities and promises that others associate with an individual professional, managed deliberately in the way firms manage product brands. It exists in the perception of others, so it is built through consistent behaviour and visible contribution rather than through self-description alone.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Peters, T. (1997) 'The brand called you', Fast Company, 10, pp. 83–90.

Glossary 4.2

Professional Narrative

The story that connects your past choices to your next move

A professional narrative is a coherent, selective account of a person's experience that links past choices, present capabilities and future direction into a meaningful sequence. Narrative identity research shows that people make sense of themselves through such stories, and in a career context the narrative gives recruiters and stakeholders a reason to remember and believe the claims.

Professional reference · Harvard style

McAdams, D.P. (2001) 'The psychology of life stories', Review of General Psychology, 5(2), pp. 100–122.

Glossary 4.3

Elevator Pitch

A brief, structured spoken case designed to earn a next conversation

An elevator pitch is a short spoken presentation, typically 30 to 90 seconds, that states who you are or what you propose, the problem or value involved, and the specific next step you seek. Its purpose is not to close a decision but to create enough interest and credibility for the listener to want a longer conversation.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Elsbach, K.D. (2003) 'How to pitch a brilliant idea', Harvard Business Review, 81(9), pp. 117–123.

Glossary 4.4

Personal Value Proposition

The fit between what you offer and what an employer needs

A personal value proposition describes how an individual's capabilities relieve specific pains and create specific gains for a defined audience, such as an employer or client. Adapted from the value proposition canvas, it forces the candidate to start from the audience's jobs and problems rather than from a list of personal attributes.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Osterwalder, A., Pigneur, Y., Bernarda, G. and Smith, A. (2014) Value Proposition Design. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.

Glossary 4.5

Digital Reputation

The impression of you formed by what is findable online

Digital reputation is the perception of an individual formed by the content that is visible about them online, including self-published profiles, contributions, endorsements and material posted by others. Online personal branding manages this reputation deliberately, and it requires consistency between the online and offline self to remain credible.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Labrecque, L.I., Markos, E. and Milne, G.R. (2011) 'Online personal branding: processes, challenges, and implications', Journal of Interactive Marketing, 25(1), pp. 37–50.

Glossary 4.6

Assertion–Evidence Structure

A slide headline that states a claim, supported by visual evidence

The assertion–evidence structure is a slide design in which a full-sentence headline states the main claim of the slide and the body supports it with visual evidence, such as a photograph, diagram or chart, rather than bullet lists. It improves audience comprehension because each slide carries one explicit message.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Alley, M. (2013) The Craft of Scientific Presentations. 2nd edn. New York: Springer.

Glossary 4.7

Nonverbal Communication

Meaning carried by voice, face and body rather than words

Nonverbal communication is the transmission of meaning through vocal tone, facial expression, posture, gesture and use of space. Mehrabian's experiments showed that when words and nonverbal cues about feelings conflict, listeners rely heavily on the nonverbal cues; the findings apply to attitude and liking and are often wrongly generalised to all communication.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Mehrabian, A. (1971) Silent Messages. Belmont, CA: Wadsworth.

Glossary 4.8

Anxiety Reappraisal

Relabelling pre-performance nerves as excitement

Anxiety reappraisal is an emotion-regulation strategy in which a person interprets the physiological arousal felt before a performance as excitement rather than trying to calm down. Because anxiety and excitement share high arousal, reappraising towards excitement is easier than suppression and has been shown to improve performance in speaking and negotiation tasks.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Brooks, A.W. (2014) 'Get excited: reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement', Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(3), pp. 1144–1158.

5Communicate with Impact: Executive Presence & Business Communication

Learning focus · ILO 5 · Message Clinic

Critically review the communication process, stakeholder communication and communication in diverse work groups.

Glossary 5.1

Pyramid Principle

Lead with the answer, then support it with grouped, logical reasons

The Pyramid Principle is a method for structuring business thinking and writing in which the governing idea is stated first and is supported by a small number of grouped arguments, each of which is in turn supported by evidence. Ideas at any level must summarise the ideas grouped below them, which makes the logic easy to follow and to challenge.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Minto, B. (1996) The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking. London: Minto International.

Glossary 5.2

Executive Presence

The combination of gravitas, communication and appearance that signals leadership readiness

Executive presence is the perceived capacity to command a room and inspire confidence, commonly described through three dimensions: gravitas (how you act), communication (how you speak and listen) and appearance (how you look). It is a perception held by others, and it can be developed through deliberate practice in each dimension.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Hewlett, S.A. (2014) Executive Presence: The Missing Link Between Merit and Success. New York: HarperBusiness.

Glossary 5.3

Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)

State the conclusion or request in the first lines

Bottom Line Up Front is a writing convention in which the conclusion, recommendation or required action is stated at the very beginning of a message, followed by the supporting detail. It serves busy readers who decide within the first sentences whether to act, and it originates in military correspondence standards.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Department of the Army (2020) Army Regulation 25-50: Preparing and Managing Correspondence. Washington, DC: Headquarters, Department of the Army.

Glossary 5.4

Executive Summary

A stand-alone digest that lets a reader decide without reading the whole

An executive summary is a short, self-contained section at the front of a business document that states the purpose, main findings, conclusions and recommendations so that a decision-maker can act without reading the full text. It is written for the reader's decision, not as a chronological preview of the report's contents.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Doumont, J.-L. (2009) Trees, Maps, and Theorems: Effective Communication for Rational Minds. Kraainem: Principiae.

Glossary 5.5

Argument (Toulmin)

A claim supported by grounds, linked by a warrant and qualified

In Toulmin's model an argument consists of a claim, the grounds or data that support it, the warrant that connects grounds to claim, backing for the warrant, a qualifier indicating the strength of the claim, and a rebuttal acknowledging exceptions. It is used to test whether a business case actually justifies its recommendation.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Toulmin, S.E. (1958) The Uses of Argument. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

Glossary 5.6

Communication Noise

Anything that distorts a message between sender and receiver

Noise is any interference that alters a message between its source and its destination, so that the signal received differs from the signal sent. In organisational communication the concept extends beyond technical interference to semantic, cultural and psychological distortions, and it explains why feedback loops are needed to confirm understanding.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Shannon, C.E. and Weaver, W. (1949) The Mathematical Theory of Communication. Urbana, IL: University of Illinois Press.

Glossary 5.7

High- and Low-Context Communication

How much meaning is carried by context rather than explicit words

High-context communication relies heavily on shared context, relationships and implicit cues, so much of the meaning is not stated; low-context communication places most of the meaning in explicit, precise words. The distinction explains frequent misunderstandings in diverse work groups, where one side hears vagueness and the other hears bluntness.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Hall, E.T. (1976) Beyond Culture. New York: Anchor Press/Doubleday.

Glossary 5.8

Cultural Dimensions

Comparable scales on which national cultures differ in values

Cultural dimensions are statistically derived scales for comparing national cultures, including power distance, individualism–collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity–femininity and long-term orientation. They describe tendencies at the level of societies and help professionals anticipate differences in communication and decision-making, while not predicting any single individual's behaviour.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Hofstede, G. (2001) Culture's Consequences: Comparing Values, Behaviors, Institutions and Organizations Across Nations. 2nd edn. Thousand Oaks, CA: Sage.

6Deliver Results: Project Management Fundamentals

Learning focus · ILOs 2 & 3 · Project Simulation

Critically assess the roles and responsibilities of a project manager as part of an organization's strategy. Investigate how to select, develop, plan, schedule and measure projects outcomes and risks.

Glossary 6.1

Project

A temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result

A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Its temporary nature means it has a defined beginning and end, and its uniqueness means it cannot be run purely as routine operations; value is delivered through outcomes that the organisation uses after the project closes.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Project Management Institute (2021) A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK Guide). 7th edn. Newtown Square, PA: PMI.

Glossary 6.2

Project Scope

The work required, and only the work required, to deliver the result

Project scope is the defined boundary of the work to be performed to deliver a product, service or result with the specified features and functions. Scope management ensures that the project includes all the work required, and only that work, which is why uncontrolled additions, known as scope creep, threaten time, cost and quality.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Project Management Institute (2021) A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK Guide). 7th edn. Newtown Square, PA: PMI.

Glossary 6.3

Waterfall Methodology

Sequential phases, each completed and signed off before the next

The waterfall methodology is a plan-driven approach in which a project moves through sequential phases, typically requirements, design, build, test and deployment, with each phase completed before the next begins. It suits projects with stable, well-understood requirements; Royce himself warned that a purely one-pass sequence is risky without feedback between phases.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Royce, W.W. (1970) 'Managing the development of large software systems', Proceedings of IEEE WESCON, August, pp. 1–9.

Glossary 6.4

Agile Methodology

Iterative delivery that values responding to change over following a plan

Agile is a family of iterative, incremental approaches to delivery that value individuals and interactions, working results, customer collaboration and responding to change. Work is delivered in short cycles with frequent feedback, which suits projects where requirements are uncertain or expected to evolve.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Beck, K. et al. (2001) Manifesto for Agile Software Development. Available at: https://agilemanifesto.org (Accessed: 16 September 2026).

Glossary 6.5

Hybrid Project Management

Combining plan-driven and agile practices according to the risk profile

Hybrid project management combines plan-driven and agile practices within one project, selecting each according to the project's risks, such as requirement stability, team size, criticality and culture. It treats methodology as a design decision rather than a doctrine, for example a fixed-scope contract phase followed by iterative delivery.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Boehm, B. and Turner, R. (2004) Balancing Agility and Discipline: A Guide for the Perplexed. Boston, MA: Addison-Wesley.

Glossary 6.6

Stakeholder

Any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the project

A stakeholder is any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of an organisation's or project's objectives. Stakeholder management identifies these parties, assesses their power and interest, and plans engagement so that support is built and resistance is anticipated rather than discovered late.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Freeman, R.E. (1984) Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach. Boston, MA: Pitman.

Glossary 6.7

Project Risk

The effect of uncertainty on objectives, positive or negative

Risk is the effect of uncertainty on objectives, where the effect may be positive (an opportunity) or negative (a threat). Project risk management identifies, analyses, evaluates and treats these uncertainties, usually recording them in a risk register with probability, impact, owner and response.

Professional reference · Harvard style

International Organization for Standardization (2018) ISO 31000:2018 Risk Management — Guidelines. Geneva: ISO.

Glossary 6.8

Sustainable Project Management

Managing projects with explicit regard for economic, social and environmental impact

Sustainable project management is the planning, monitoring and control of project delivery and support processes with consideration of the environmental, economic and social aspects of the life cycle of the project's resources, processes, deliverables and effects. It widens project success beyond time, cost and quality to include long-term and societal outcomes.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Silvius, A.J.G. and Schipper, R.P.J. (2014) 'Sustainability in project management: a literature review and impact analysis', Social Business, 4(1), pp. 63–96.

7Negotiate & Influence: Getting What You Need Without Formal Authority

Learning focus · ILO 4 · Negotiation Simulation

Evaluate the keys to successful negotiation and differentiate negotiation styles, strategies, and tactics.

Glossary 7.1

Negotiation

Joint decision-making by parties with partly conflicting interests

Negotiation is a process by which two or more interdependent parties who have both shared and conflicting interests seek to reach an agreement that each prefers to the outcome of not agreeing. It is chosen over unilateral action when parties need each other to achieve their goals.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Lewicki, R.J., Barry, B. and Saunders, D.M. (2020) Negotiation. 8th edn. New York: McGraw-Hill Education.

Glossary 7.2

BATNA

Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: your walk-away option

A BATNA is the best course of action available to a party if the current negotiation fails to produce an agreement. It is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured, and improving it is the most reliable way to increase negotiating power.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Fisher, R., Ury, W. and Patton, B. (2011) Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In. 3rd edn. New York: Penguin Books.

Glossary 7.3

ZOPA

Zone Of Possible Agreement: the overlap between the parties' reservation points

The ZOPA is the range of outcomes that both parties would prefer to no agreement, bounded by each side's reservation value, the least favourable terms they would accept. If the reservation values do not overlap there is no ZOPA and no deal is rational; if they do, negotiation determines where in the zone the agreement falls.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Raiffa, H. (1982) The Art and Science of Negotiation. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.

Glossary 7.4

Principled Negotiation

The Harvard Method: interests, options, criteria, and people separated from problem

Principled negotiation, known as the Harvard Method, decides issues on their merits through four principles: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria. It aims at wise agreements reached efficiently without damaging the relationship.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Fisher, R., Ury, W. and Patton, B. (2011) Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In. 3rd edn. New York: Penguin Books.

Glossary 7.5

Distributive vs. Integrative Negotiation

Dividing a fixed pie versus expanding it

Distributive negotiation concerns the division of a fixed amount of value, so one party's gain is the other's loss; integrative negotiation seeks agreements that increase joint value by trading across issues the parties value differently. Most real negotiations contain both elements, and the skill lies in recognising which one dominates at each moment.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Walton, R.E. and McKersie, R.B. (1965) A Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations. New York: McGraw-Hill.

Glossary 7.6

Influence Without Authority

Gaining cooperation through exchange rather than hierarchy

Influence without authority is the ability to obtain cooperation from people over whom one has no formal power, based on the law of reciprocity. The influencer identifies what the other party values, their currencies, such as information, recognition or support, and offers something of value in exchange for what is needed.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Cohen, A.R. and Bradford, D.L. (2005) Influence Without Authority. 2nd edn. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.

Glossary 7.7

Tactical Empathy

Deliberately understanding and naming the other side's emotions

Tactical empathy is the deliberate effort to understand the feelings and perspective of a counterpart and to voice that understanding, for example through labelling and mirroring, in order to influence the course of a negotiation. It does not require agreement or sympathy; it builds the trust and information flow on which agreement depends.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Voss, C. (2016) Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It. New York: HarperBusiness.

Glossary 7.8

Manipulation

Influence that bypasses a person's capacity for reflective choice

Manipulation is a form of influence that does not sufficiently engage or appeal to a person's capacity for reflection and deliberation, for example by exploiting biases, concealing relevant information or creating false urgency. It marks the ethical boundary of negotiation: persuasion respects the counterpart's agency, manipulation undermines it.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Sunstein, C.R. (2016) The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science. New York: Cambridge University Press.

Glossary 8.1

Energy Management

Managing physical, emotional, mental and spiritual capacity, not just hours

Energy management is an approach to personal productivity that treats energy, rather than time, as the fundamental currency of performance. It builds rituals that renew four dimensions of capacity: physical, emotional, mental and spiritual, on the premise that time is finite while energy can be systematically expanded and renewed.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Schwartz, T. and McCarthy, C. (2007) 'Manage your energy, not your time', Harvard Business Review, 85(10), pp. 63–73.

Glossary 8.2

Eisenhower Matrix

Sorting tasks by urgency and importance

The Eisenhower Matrix is a prioritisation tool that classifies tasks on two dimensions, urgency and importance, into four quadrants: do now, schedule, delegate and eliminate. Its central insight is that important but non-urgent work, such as planning and development, is the quadrant most often neglected and most valuable to protect.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Covey, S.R. (1989) The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People. New York: Free Press.

Glossary 8.3

OKRs

Objectives and Key Results: an ambitious goal and its measurable evidence

Objectives and Key Results is a goal-setting method in which an objective states what is to be achieved and a small set of key results states, in measurable terms, how progress towards it will be verified. Applied at the personal level, OKRs connect weekly effort to a small number of priorities and make progress visible.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Doerr, J. (2018) Measure What Matters. New York: Portfolio/Penguin.

Glossary 8.4

Prompt Literacy

The ability to instruct an AI system clearly and evaluate what it returns

Prompt literacy is the ability to formulate instructions for a generative AI system, specifying role, task, context, format, constraints and examples, and to iterate and evaluate the outputs critically. It draws on reusable prompt patterns, and it includes knowing when a prompt cannot compensate for missing expertise or unreliable output.

Professional reference · Harvard style

White, J. et al. (2023) 'A prompt pattern catalog to enhance prompt engineering with ChatGPT', arXiv preprint arXiv:2302.11382.

Glossary 8.5

Human-in-the-Loop

Keeping a person responsible for checking and owning AI-supported outcomes

Human-in-the-loop describes a working arrangement in which a person remains involved in, and accountable for, the decisions an AI system supports: setting the task, verifying the output and owning the result. Design guidelines stress that people must be able to understand, correct and dismiss AI behaviour for this oversight to be real.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Amershi, S. et al. (2019) 'Guidelines for human-AI interaction', Proceedings of the 2019 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems. New York: ACM, pp. 1–13.

Glossary 8.6

Automation vs. Augmentation

Letting the machine take over a task, or using it to amplify the person

Automation means machines take over a task from humans, while augmentation means humans collaborate closely with machines to perform a task. The two are interdependent across time and levels of the organisation, so an exclusive focus on either one produces unintended consequences, such as de-skilling or lost efficiency.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Raisch, S. and Krakowski, S. (2021) 'Artificial intelligence and management: the automation–augmentation paradox', Academy of Management Review, 46(1), pp. 192–210.

Glossary 8.7

Jagged Technological Frontier

AI performs well on some tasks and poorly on similar-looking ones

The jagged technological frontier describes the uneven boundary of AI capability: tasks of apparently similar difficulty may fall inside the frontier, where AI improves quality and speed, or outside it, where relying on AI makes results worse. Because the boundary is invisible, professionals must test and verify rather than assume.

Professional reference · Harvard style

Dell'Acqua, F. et al. (2023) 'Navigating the jagged technological frontier: field experimental evidence of the effects of AI on knowledge worker productivity and quality', Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013.

Glossary 8.8

AI Attribution

Transparent disclosure of how AI contributed to a piece of work

AI attribution is the practice of disclosing whether, where and how generative AI was used in producing academic or professional work, so that readers and assessors can judge authorship and reliability. It is part of academic integrity and responsible use, alongside verifying outputs and protecting personal data.

Professional reference · Harvard style

UNESCO (2023) Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research. Paris: UNESCO.
